Peer comparisons: balancing learning and potential disengagement

Comparing performance can support improvement, but identifying peers may foster competition, defensiveness, or unfavorable perceptions of feedback—and may discourage engagement (Chun et al., 2023, DOI; Vilendrer et al., 2021, DOI).

Recommendations

  • Start anonymously. Use random identifiers for the first improvement cycle. Do not upload identifiable personnel or patient information to this public demonstration website. Anonymity does not prevent learning from positive deviants. See below about confidentially interviewing positive deviants
  • Consider identifying positive deviants only in a subsequent improvement cycle. In an organization-approved setting, consider an anonymous vote seeking broad participant support, followed by permission from each named person or unit. Identification can facilitate reciprocal learning (Ivanovic et al., 2015, DOI).
  • Avoid identifying everyone. Naming lower-performing peers may undermine collaboration, even when participants agree to disclosure.
  • Seek transferable practice efficiencies, not just hard work. Interview positive deviants privately, even when plots are anonymous. Some achieve better results through additional effort; others develop more efficient workflows. Investigate and test the practices before spreading them (Baxter et al., 2016, DOI; O'Malley et al., 2022, DOI).
  • Make feedback actionable. Investigate distinctive tactics independently or during a prompt, supportive positive deviance seminar. Focus on workable improvements, not rankings (Ivanovic et al., 2018, DOI; Hysong, 2009, DOI).

Data privacy: Selecting “No names” hides labels on the plot, but does not remove names from data sent to the external OpenCPU demonstration server. Replace names with random identifiers before uploading. Do not upload identifiable patient information.
